HepAus against published Australian hepatitis B models

How the Atomica-based HepAus model differs structurally from the three main published dynamic-population models of chronic hepatitis B (CHB) burden in Australia.

Headline difference: HepAus is a genuine person-to-person transmission model (force-of-infection driven, with feedback between prevalence and future incidence). All three comparison models are disease-progression / cascade models — they project prevalence forward from assumed incidence and migration inputs, without a transmission mechanism that can respond to changes in exposure.

Models compared

HepAus this model

Burnet Institute, Atomica framework

Dynamic transmission model, 24 populations (4 origin groups × 3 age bands × 2 sexes), calibrated via Y-factor optimisation to HBsAg/HBeAg prevalence and mortality data.

McCulloch et al. 2020

Hepatology 71:1170–1181

National CHB burden and elimination-progress model. Disease-progression only, 18 age bands, stratified by country of birth, dynamic migration.

McCulloch et al. 2023

Aust NZ J Public Health 47(1)

Jurisdictional (state/territory) extension of the 2020 model, same core structure, used to compare cascade-of-care gaps across jurisdictions.

Xiao / Howell / van Gemert et al. 2020

J Viral Hepatitis 27:526–536

Markov cost-effectiveness model of the care cascade, used to estimate DALYs and ICERs of scaling up diagnosis, linkage, and treatment.

Why the transmission mechanism matters

Because McCulloch 2020/2023 and Xiao 2020 do not model transmission, their future prevalence trajectories are driven almost entirely by assumed incidence: migration of already-infected people, and a fixed background rate of new infection. They cannot represent an intervention that reduces exposure — e.g. how reduced prevalence among 15–64-year-olds today lowers horizontal transmission risk to children a decade later. HepAus's force-of-infection structure (foi_cal, horiz_chb, interaction matrices) makes this feedback explicit, at the cost of needing a genuine calibration procedure (rather than literature-derived point estimates) to pin down transmission intensity per population.

Why the Indigenous stratum matters

McCulloch et al. explicitly state that "specific modelling of CHB in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians" was not included in their primary analysis, citing insufficient age-specific prevalence data, and describe a dedicated Indigenous model as ongoing future work. HepAus's four-origin-group structure treats ATSI as a first-class population from the outset, with its own demography, natural history calibration and cascade-of-care parameters — directly addressing the gap those authors flag, though at the cost of needing Indigenous-specific input data of a quality the national model could not assume.

Where the comparison models are more detailed

  • Age resolution: McCulloch's 18 five-year age bands resolve age-specific cirrhosis/HCC risk far more finely than HepAus's three broad bands.
  • Country-of-birth granularity: McCulloch separately models China, Vietnam, Philippines and Taiwan (>45% of migrants with CHB), whereas HepAus collapses all overseas-born people into two risk tiers (low/high).
  • Uncertainty quantification: McCulloch's Latin hypercube sampling across 1,000 runs produces formal plausible ranges around every output; HepAus's current calibration produces point estimates from a single optimised parameter set.

Summary

The three published models sit in the same family as each other — disease-progression or decision-analytic cascade models, calibrated from literature and administrative data (NNDSS notifications, PBS dispensing, Medicare), used to track elimination-target progress or cost-effectiveness. HepAus is structurally different: it is the only one of the four with an explicit transmission mechanism and an explicit Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander stratum, trading finer age and country-of-birth resolution for those two capabilities.
